How to resize an image

Resizing changes an image's dimensions in pixels — its width and height — rather than just its file size. Sites ask for exact dimensions all the time: a 1080×1080 profile or post image, a 1200×630 share image, a passport-style photo, a product picture that must be at least 1000 px on its longest side.

Step by step

1. Open the Image Resizer. The image is processed in your browser, never uploaded.

2. Choose your image. The tool shows its current dimensions, so you know where you're starting from.

3. Enter the target width and height in pixels — exactly what the site or form asked for.

4. Preview and download. Check the preview: if faces look stretched or squashed, the target shape doesn't match your photo's shape — see the aspect-ratio note below.

The one idea that matters: aspect ratio

Aspect ratio is the relationship between width and height — a 4000×3000 photo is 4:3. If you force it into a square (1:1) by typing 1000×1000, something has to give: either the image stretches (people look wrong) or parts get cut off. When a platform wants a square and your photo isn't, crop it to shape first, then resize the crop to the exact pixels.

Tips that save you a re-do

Smaller is easy, bigger is fake. Shrinking an image always works well. Enlarging one invents detail that doesn't exist, so it comes out soft. If a form demands a minimum size your photo doesn't reach, a slightly soft enlarged copy is still usually accepted — but don't expect it to look sharp.

Match the longest side for listings. Marketplaces often say “at least 1000 px”. Resizing so the longest edge is 1600–2000 px gives zoom headroom without a giant file.

Resize, then compress. After resizing, run the result through the Image Compressor at quality 80 — dimensions and compression together give the smallest clean file.

Frequently asked questions

What size should a social media post image be?

Square posts: 1080×1080. Portrait posts: 1080×1350. Stories and reels covers: 1080×1920. Link-share images: 1200×630. These are the sizes the platforms display natively, so nothing gets re-scaled or cropped unexpectedly.

Does resizing reduce the file size too?

Yes, usually a lot — fewer pixels means fewer bytes. Halving both dimensions quarters the pixel count. But resizing and compressing are different levers: resizing changes dimensions, compression changes how efficiently those pixels are stored. Demanding forms may check either or both.

Why does my resized image look blurry?

Almost always because it was enlarged beyond its original size, or because it will be displayed larger than it is. Keep the output at or below the original dimensions whenever sharpness matters.
