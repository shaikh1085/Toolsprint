How to compress an image

“File too large” is one of the most common dead ends on the internet — an email limit, an application form, a shop upload. Compression re-encodes the photo so it takes less space, trading a little image detail (often detail nobody can see) for a much smaller file.

Step by step

1. Open the Image Compressor. Your photo is processed in your browser and never uploaded anywhere.

2. Choose your image. JPG, PNG and WebP all work. The tool shows the original size.

3. Set the quality. Start at 80. For most photos that's dramatically smaller and visually identical. Only go lower if you need a specific size limit.

4. Compare, then download. The tool shows “Original → New (percent saved)” with a preview. If the new file is somehow bigger, it warns you — keep the original in that case.

Choosing the right quality

85–95: barely smaller, safest for photos you'll print or archive.

75–85: the sweet spot for websites, emails and uploads — big savings, no visible difference on screen.

50–70: for thumbnails and previews where small size matters more than detail.

Below 50: expect visible softness and blocky patches. Only for emergencies.

Tips that save you a re-do

Resize first, compress second. A 4000-pixel photo shown at 800 pixels wastes most of its bytes on pixels nobody sees. Shrink the dimensions with the Image Resizer, then compress — the combined saving is far bigger than either alone.

PNG is for graphics, not photos. A PNG photo can be 10× the size of the same photo as JPG with nothing to show for it. Screenshots with text and logos are the opposite — they stay sharp as PNG. Logos and screenshots: PNG. Photographs: JPG or WebP.

Never compress the same file repeatedly. Each save at a lossy quality throws away a bit more. Always compress from your original, and keep the original untouched.

Frequently asked questions

Will people be able to tell my photo was compressed?

At quality 80, essentially no — the differences sit below what eyes notice on a screen. Zooming into fine detail (hair, fabric, distant text) is where heavy compression shows first, which is why printed photos deserve a higher setting.

Why did my compressed image come out bigger?

It happens when the original was already efficiently encoded — for example a WebP saved by another tool. Re-encoding it as JPG at high quality can add bytes instead of removing them. The tool warns you when this happens; just keep your original.

What is the fastest way under a strict limit, like 200 KB?

Resize the photo down (long edge around 1600 px is plenty for forms), then compress at quality 75. Check the result size and nudge the quality down in small steps until you are under the limit.
